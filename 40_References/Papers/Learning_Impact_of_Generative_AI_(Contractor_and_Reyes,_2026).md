# Experimental evidence on the learning impact of generative AI (Contractor and Reyes, 2026)

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Question: if undergraduates learn an unfamiliar topic with an off-the-shelf generative AI available, do they know more afterwards than students who learn without it?
> Method: randomized controlled experiment in proctored, in-person sessions, with unaided knowledge tests and essays immediately and one week later.
> Result: AI access raised immediate test scores by 0.27 standard deviations, and the one-week lead was 3.9 percentage points (0.21 standard deviations) with p = 0.068, which is short of statistical significance.

## Study explanation

The problem here is narrow and worth stating precisely. Students now sit with a generative AI open next to their coursework, and teachers argue about whether that is studying or outsourcing. Contractor and Reyes (2026) turn part of that argument into a measurement: if undergraduates learn an unfamiliar topic with an off-the-shelf generative AI available, do they know more afterwards than students who learn the same topic without it? This is an IZA Discussion Paper (number 18792, July 2026, also posted on arXiv as 2607.08849), so the evidence is provisional and should be read as provisional.

The design is a randomized controlled experiment, and the randomization happened in proctored, in-person sessions. That detail matters more than it first appears. If students could take the AI home, you could never separate what they learned in the session from what they learned later on their own. Undergraduates were assigned to learn an unfamiliar topic and then write an analytical essay, either with the AI available or without it. Afterward everyone took unaided assessments: immediately after the session and again one week later. Learning was measured two ways, with knowledge tests covering factual and conceptual understanding, and with open-ended essays for higher-order skills.

A worked example with the reported numbers. Suppose you are reading the results table. The AI group's immediate knowledge test lands 0.27 standard deviations above the no-AI group's. A standard deviation is simply the typical spread of scores among students, so 0.27 means the gap was roughly a quarter of the distance you would normally expect between one student's score and another's. That is a real and noticeable difference on the test taken right after the session. One week later, the same comparison gives a lead of 3.9 percentage points, which is 0.21 standard deviations, with p = 0.068. Read that p value carefully: if the two groups were truly identical, a gap this large or larger would turn up by chance roughly 7 times out of 100. The estimate falls short of statistical significance. It is not proof that the effect is zero, and it is not proof of a durable effect either.

The essays tell a more interesting story than the tests do. While students still had AI access, essay quality changed little. One week later, when they wrote again without AI, quality had improved in style and relevance. Split by behavior, the delayed gains were larger among augmentation users, students who used the AI to explain concepts rather than to generate text. The short-run quality gains of automation users, students who let the AI write for them, vanished once the AI was removed. Two mechanisms are proposed behind the gains: students shifted time away from drafting text and toward reading and searching for information, and they reported greater learning enjoyment with the AI present. Enjoyment is self-reported, so treat it as a plausible contributor rather than a measured mediator.

The most common mistake is to read 0.27 standard deviations as "27 percent better" or as 27 points of exam score. It is neither. The second mistake, which the headline invites, is to collapse the two time points into a single verdict such as "AI improves learning". The immediate effect is solid and significant; the one-week effect is smaller and does not reach significance. People make this mistake because the two numbers are printed in different units, 0.27 standard deviations against 3.9 percentage points, and different units invite different mental stories. One more trap: p = 0.068 does not mean the delayed effect is zero. It means this experiment did not have the precision to tell.

[[Retrieval_Practice|Retrieval practice]] is the reference point: the condition that benefited most at one week was the one where students used the AI to understand concepts rather than to outsource the writing, which sits much closer to asking than to answering. Read the Limitations below before carrying any of this forward, and remember that a discussion paper is evidence in progress.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** AI access raised immediate test scores by 0.27 standard deviations in a randomized in-person session, and the one-week lead of 3.9 percentage points (0.21 standard deviations) was short of significance with p = 0.068. The delayed gains were larger among augmentation users, while automation users' short-run quality gains disappeared once AI was removed.
- **Common confusion:** a standard deviation is not a percentage of the score. People also read p = 0.068 as proof that AI has no lasting effect, when it only means the experiment was not precise enough to tell.
- **Exercise:** write down in one sentence why 3.9 percentage points is not comparable with 0.27 standard deviations, and say which of the two the one-week estimate is.

## Results

| Outcome | Result with AI access |
|---|---|
| Immediate knowledge test | +0.27 standard deviations |
| One-week knowledge test | 3.9 percentage points ahead (0.21 standard deviations), p = 0.068 |
| Essay quality while AI was available | changed little |
| Essay quality one week later, unaided | improved in style and relevance |
| Larger delayed gains | augmentation users, who used AI to explain concepts |
| Short-run quality gains | automation users, who let AI write for them; gone once AI was removed |

## Limitations

- IZA discussion paper: provisional evidence, not peer reviewed.
- One unfamiliar topic, one proctored in-person session, undergraduates only.
- The longest follow-up measured is one week.
- The one-week estimate is not statistically significant (p = 0.068).
- Learning enjoyment is self-reported, so the mechanism is plausible rather than measured.
- Off-the-shelf AI only; no tutor prompt design or course integration was tested.
