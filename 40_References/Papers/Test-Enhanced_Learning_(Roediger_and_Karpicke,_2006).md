# Test-enhanced learning (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006)

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Question: does taking a memory test help you remember more than studying again?
> Method: students read prose passages, then either reread them or took free-recall tests without feedback; retention was measured after 5 minutes, 2 days and 1 week.
> Result: rereading won at 5 minutes (83% vs 71%), testing won at one week (61% vs 40%).

## Study explanation

Students often reread a text to prepare for an exam, because rereading feels productive: the page looks familiar and nothing hurts. The question behind this paper is whether that feeling predicts what you will remember later. The authors compare rereading with taking a memory test on the same material, and they measure memory after different delays.

The design is simple, which is part of why the paper became the standard citation for the testing effect. Students read short prose passages. Then, depending on the condition, they either studied the passage again or tried to write down everything they could remember, on a blank sheet. No feedback followed the tests, so any benefit cannot come from seeing corrections. It must come from the act of retrieval itself. Retention was then measured after 5 minutes, 2 days and 1 week.

A worked example with the numbers. Take the group that kept rereading and the group that kept testing. After 5 minutes, rereaders recalled 83% of the passage and the testers 71%, so rereading looked better. After 1 week, the order had flipped: testers recalled 61% and rereaders 40%. Read only the first number and you would tell students to reread. Read the second and you would tell them to test themselves. The difference between the two readings is the delay, and in real study the delay is days, not minutes.

The most common mistake is to take the 5-minute result as the whole story. It is the result a student sees when they judge their own learning right after studying, and it points the wrong way for anything that must last. A second mistake is to read the paper as saying that studying again is useless. It says that, in this setting, tests produced more durable memory than repeated reading. It does not say that the first reading is optional: students still had to read the passage once before anything could be recalled.

The paper connects to two other notes. [[Retrieval_Practice|Retrieval practice]] turns the finding into a habit: recall before you look. [[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]] uses it as the first piece of evidence for why an assistant should ask you questions instead of handing you answers. Before you generalize, read the Limitations section below: the material was prose, the test was free recall, and one week is the longest delay measured. The practical lesson for this vault is short: write from memory before you open a summary, then compare.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** short-term performance and long-term retention can point in opposite directions.
- **Common confusion:** the effect does not need feedback to appear, although feedback helps in practice.
- **Exercise:** explain the crossover result to someone in two sentences.

## Results

| Delay | Restudy | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 5 minutes | 83% | 71% |
| 1 week | 40% | 61% |

## Limitations

- Prose passages and free recall; one week is the longest delay measured here.
- Undergraduate participants in a lab setting.
