# Test-enhanced learning (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006)

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Question: does taking a memory test help you remember more than studying again?
> Method: students read prose passages, then either reread them or took free-recall tests without feedback; retention was measured after 5 minutes, 2 days and 1 week.
> Result: rereading won at 5 minutes (83% vs 71%), testing won at one week (61% vs 40%).

## Study explanation

This paper is the standard citation for the testing effect: taking a test on material improves later retention more than spending the same time restudying it. The design is simple, which is part of why it became influential. Students studied short passages and then either studied them again or tried to recall them on a blank sheet. No feedback was given, so the benefit cannot come from seeing corrections; it comes from the act of retrieval itself.

The interesting part is the crossover. Right after learning, rereading looks better, so students judging by how they feel will choose it. After a delay, the advantage reverses. That is why the authors argue that testing is a tool for learning, not only for assessment. The practical lesson for this vault: write from memory before you open a summary.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** short-term performance and long-term retention can point in opposite directions.
- **Common confusion:** the effect does not need feedback to appear, although feedback helps in practice.
- **Exercise:** explain the crossover result to someone in two sentences.

## Results

| Delay | Restudy | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 5 minutes | 83% | 71% |
| 1 week | 40% | 61% |

## Limitations

- Prose passages and free recall; one week is the longest delay measured here.
- Undergraduate participants in a lab setting.
