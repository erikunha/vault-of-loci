# The method of loci in the context of psychological research: a systematic review and meta-analysis (Ondrej, 2025)

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Question: how well does the method of loci, placing items along a familiar route, beat plain rehearsal for remembering ordered lists?
> Method: systematic review following PRISMA and PICO, with risk of bias assessed by RoBMAPSMA and certainty of evidence by GRADE.
> Result: placing items along a route reliably beats rehearsing them for ordered lists, but the size of the benefit is uncertain, with a pooled d of 0.88 that two small-study corrections disagree about.

## Study explanation

The method of loci is one of the oldest memory techniques in the book: you attach each item to a fixed place along a route you know well, then walk the route in your head to recall the list in order. It is taught widely and believed widely. Ondrej (2025) asks how well it actually works, and does it the way a careful reader should, by collecting the experiments systematically rather than by picking the impressive ones. The review follows PRISMA and PICO, and it grades its own evidence twice, once for risk of bias with RoBMAPSMA and once for certainty of the body of evidence with GRADE. That grading is the most valuable part of the paper, because it tells you how much to trust the headline number.

A worked example with the reported numbers. Suppose you have a list of items to recall in order. Under rehearsal you repeat the list until it feels smooth. Under the method of loci you place each item at a spot on a familiar route and walk it. Pooling the experiments in adults, the loci group recalled better by d = 0.88, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.47 to 1.25. Read that as: the gap between the two methods was about 0.88 of the typical spread of recall scores, and the plausible range is wide, from roughly half that gap to well above it. The pooled sample was about 3,006 participants aged 17 to 88, and in younger adults the effect was medium, d = 0.75. The Bayesian numbers point the same way as the frequentist ones: the Bayes factor was 161.94 and P(M|data) = 0.994, which is the data's strong preference for the model with a real effect over the model without one.

Now the part that should change what you do with the number. The authors rated the certainty of the evidence as low to very low. 89.2 percent of the experiments were at high risk of bias. There was very strong evidence of moderate heterogeneity, so the experiments are not measuring one thing, and very strong evidence of publication bias, so the published set is likely the favorable part of the literature. There were also indications of a small-study effect, and when the authors applied two standard corrections for it, they disagreed completely: one correction shrank the pooled effect to almost nothing (d = 0.04), and the other produced an implausibly large estimate. A correction that removes the entire effect and another that inflates it past anything believable are both signs that the original estimate is not trustworthy.

The most common mistake is to quote d = 0.88 and stop. It reads as a settled fact, and it is not: the same paper says the evidence is low to very low quality, and a single study correction reduces the effect to 0.04. The second mistake is to confuse the domain. The effect is about remembering ordered lists, usually word lists, usually learned and tested on the same day, mostly by young psychology undergraduates. It is not about understanding a concept, solving a problem or applying knowledge, and it is not about remembering a semester of lecture material. A third mistake is to treat the method of loci as a replacement for studying. It is an encoding strategy for serial material, not a test.

Two notes in this vault connect. [[Retrieval_Practice|Retrieval practice]] is the practice the method of loci does not replace: the loci give you a way to lay out what you want to remember, while retrieval practice is the effortful act of pulling it out, and this review says nothing that would weaken the case for testing yourself. [[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]] is the place to check before using any of this with a tool: nothing in this review tests an AI tutor or AI-assisted use of the method of loci (not verified), so do not carry the pooled number over into claims about AI-supported study. The honest summary is that the technique reliably beats rehearsal for ordered lists in the published literature, while the size of that advantage, and how far it generalizes, remain uncertain.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** the method of loci beat rehearsal for immediate serial recall in adults with a pooled d of 0.88 (95% CI 0.47 to 1.25) and a Bayes factor of 161.94, but the authors rated the evidence low to very low, with 89.2 percent of experiments at high risk of bias and two small-study corrections that disagree, one giving d = 0.04 and the other an implausibly large estimate.
- **Common confusion:** people treat d = 0.88 as settled and treat the method of loci as a way to learn material. It is a way to remember ordered lists, and the review contains nothing about understanding or applying knowledge.
- **Exercise:** explain in two sentences why two corrections for small-study effects that disagree with each other mean the pooled estimate cannot be taken at face value.

## Limitations

- Evidence quality rated low to very low by GRADE, with 89.2 percent of experiments at high risk of bias.
- Very strong evidence of moderate heterogeneity and of publication bias, plus indications of a small-study effect.
- The two small-study corrections disagree, so the pooled size of the effect is not settled.
- Material is usually word lists, and training and testing usually happen on the same day.
- Participants are mostly young psychology undergraduates.
- The outcome is ordered serial recall, not understanding, application or long-term retention.
