# Contribution of metacognitive questions to accuracy of judgment of learning in a digital environment (Michalsky and Bakrish, 2024)

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Question: does asking metacognitive questions while studying make a student judge their own learning more accurately?
> Method: two classroom studies with sixth sessions of digital study, one on reading literacy with 65 fifth-graders and one on mathematical literacy with 72, where the experimental material included metacognitive questioning.
> Result: the experimental groups improved over the six sessions in both performance and calibration, meaning the gap between what they predicted and what they scored got smaller.

## Study explanation

Most learners know the moment they are overconfident only after the result arrives. Michalsky and Bakrish (2024) ask a more useful question: can a student be made to judge their own learning better while the learning is still happening? The mechanism they test is a question asked during the study session, not a reflection written at the end. That distinction matters, because a reflection at the end is already contaminated by having seen the material twice.

The design runs as two parallel studies, each over six sessions. In Study 1, 65 fifth-grade students worked on short texts in a reading literacy program. In Study 2, 72 students worked on stories containing mathematical components in a mathematical literacy program. Every session had the same shape: the student studied the material, then judged their comprehension by predicting their performance, and only then took the test. The prediction came before the test, so it was a real prediction and not a memory of the result. In the experimental groups, metacognitive questioning was built into the study materials themselves.

A worked example with the numbers the paper reports. Take a student whose sessions end in a prediction and a score. Calibration is the distance between the two. If the student predicts 70 and scores 70, the gap is zero, and that is calibration. If the student predicts 90 and scores 55, the gap is 35 points and the student is overconfident. Across the six sessions, the experimental groups in both studies moved in two directions at once: performance went up and calibration got better, which means the prediction gap narrowed. Both halves matter. A program that raised performance while the predictions stayed badly wrong would leave the student unable to plan the next session, and a program that improved calibration without raising performance would only have made the student accurately pessimistic.

The most common mistake is to read this as a study of testing. It is not. The prediction of performance is a judgment of learning, and the intervention is a question inside the material. The paper does not compare metacognitive questioning against retrieval practice, and it does not measure retention after a delay. The second mistake is to carry the numbers to adults, to university material or to a semester of study. The participants were fifth-graders working on short digital sessions in school subjects, and the intervention lasted six sessions.

Two notes in this vault connect. [[Retrieval_Practice|Retrieval practice]] is the practice that produces the number the judgment is compared to, and this paper does not challenge it. [[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]] is where to check before assuming that an AI tutor already asks these questions: this study tested a designed study material, not an open chat, so it is evidence that the question helps, not evidence that any assistant does it.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** metacognitive questioning inside the study material improved both performance and calibration over six sessions in two studies of fifth-grade students, 65 in reading literacy and 72 in mathematical literacy.
- **Common confusion:** people read this as a study of testing or of AI tutors. It studied a designed question inside the material, with the prediction made before the test.
- **Exercise:** explain in two sentences why asking a student to predict their score before the test measures calibration rather than memory.

## Results

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Study 1 sample | 65 fifth-grade students, reading literacy |
| Study 2 sample | 72 fifth-grade students, mathematical literacy |
| Duration | six sessions in each study |
| Judgment moment | predicted performance before the test |
| Effect in the experimental groups | improvement in performance and in calibration over the six sessions |

## Limitations

- Fifth-grade students in school literacy and mathematics programs.
- Six sessions of short digital material, not a course or a semester.
- No delayed follow-up of the judgment, so the study speaks about six sessions and not about retention.
- The intervention is a designed study material with questions in it, not a free conversation with an assistant.
