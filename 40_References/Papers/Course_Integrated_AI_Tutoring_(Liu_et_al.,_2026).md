# The effects of course-integrated AI tutoring on student performance and engagement: a randomized university trial (Liu et al., 2026)

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Question: what happens to grades and classroom engagement when a generative AI tutor is bolted onto a course that is already running?
> Method: randomized controlled trial with 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors across multiple disciplines at a large US public university, comparing sections of the same course.
> Result: tutor access reduced final grades by 0.37 standard deviations and learning management system participation by 0.90 standard deviations, so a tutor added to a course can lower grades and participation at the same time.

## Study explanation

The problem this paper attacks is the opposite of the optimistic tutor story. Plenty of work asks whether an AI tutor can help a student. This trial asks what happens to an existing course when an AI tutor is added to it. That is a different question, and it is the one a department chair actually has to answer. The authors are explicit that the result is not that AI tutors never work. It is that a tutor inserted into a course, without redesigning the course around it, can subtract.

The design is a randomized controlled trial at a large US public university, with 2,379 undergraduate students and 30 instructors across multiple disciplines. Randomization happened among sections of the same course, so the comparison is between sections that ran in parallel with the same instructor pool and the same assessment plan. That controls for a lot of what usually confuses course comparisons. Tutor access was the manipulated condition. Two outcomes carried the result: final grades and learning management system participation, the latter standing in for how much students engaged with the course's own materials.

A worked example with the numbers. Take the final grade first. Sections with tutor access scored 0.37 standard deviations below sections without it. As always with this scale, the way to read it is that the gap equals 0.37 of the typical spread of grades among students, so a meaningful but not enormous difference. Then take the engagement measure. Learning management system participation fell by 0.90 standard deviations, which is nearly a full standard deviation. That is the larger number, and it is the one to slow down on: students in the tutor sections did markedly less of what the course normally asks them to do inside the learning platform. The authors note that participation declines were similarly large in the full sample, so this is not an artifact of a subgroup, and that estimated academic losses were greater among first-generation students.

The mechanism the authors suggest is displacement. The AI tutor may take the place of engagement with existing learning activities without improving academic performance. The AI is present, the student gets a tutor, and the activities that were carrying the learning (reading, practice, discussion, submitting work) get crowded out. The mechanism is a suggestion, not a demonstrated mediator: the trial measures outcomes, not what each student did inside the tutor.

The most common mistake is to read this as "AI tutors do not work" and throw out the entire tutor literature. That is not what the trial says, and it is exactly the wrong conclusion to draw from a discussion paper (IZA number 18989, October 2026, also posted on EdWorking Papers that month). The finding is scoped to one integration design in one institution, and the authors themselves raise the possibility that impact differs across student groups. A second mistake is arithmetic: reading 0.37 standard deviations as "grades fell 37 percent". It did not. It fell by 0.37 of the natural spread of grades. A third mistake is to treat the participation drop as a side effect. It is the larger of the two effects and probably the more informative one, because it measures how much students engaged with the course's own learning activities, which is where the learning would have happened, and the note on [[Retrieval_Practice|Retrieval practice]] explains why engagement with those activities is what drives learning.

Two other notes in this vault frame the result. [[Retrieval_Practice|Retrieval practice]] says that learning comes from doing the effortful thing yourself: recall, practice, retrieve. If a tool displaces the activities that generate practice, the theory predicts exactly the pattern observed here. [[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]] sets the protocol for using an AI without paying this price: the AI asks, hints and corrects, and you recall, solve and explain. The trial did not test that protocol. It tested course integration, and the important lesson is not that AI tutoring fails but that adding a tool to a course without changing what students do can subtract value from it.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** in a randomized trial of course-integrated AI tutoring with 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors, tutor access reduced final grades by 0.37 standard deviations and learning management system participation by 0.90 standard deviations. Estimated losses were greater among first-generation students, and the authors suggest the tutor displaced engagement with existing learning activities.
- **Common confusion:** people read this as proof that AI tutors never help. The trial covers one integration design at one institution, and the participation drop is the bigger effect, not a footnote.
- **Exercise:** list three course activities that an integrated AI tutor could displace, and mark which of them are the ones this vault would call retrieval practice.

## Limitations

- IZA discussion paper: provisional evidence, not peer reviewed.
- One institution and one integration design, so the finding does not transfer automatically to a tutor built around a different course structure.
- Sections of the same course were compared, so instructor and course effects are controlled only to the extent the randomization worked.
- The displacement mechanism is proposed by the authors, not measured directly.
- No data are reported on how students used the tutor, or on which students benefited.
- Engagement is measured through learning management system participation, which is a proxy for studying, not a direct measure of learning.
