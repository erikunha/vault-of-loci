# Retrieval spacing and interleaving in classrooms (Ruitenburg et al., 2026)

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Question: do retrieval practice, distributed practice and interleaved practice still work in real classrooms, and under what conditions?
> Method: three-level meta-analysis of 233 effect sizes nested in 71 experiments nested in 53 authentic school classroom studies (2006 to 2024), with 7,303 participants.
> Result: all three work, but with smaller and more conditional effects than laboratory syntheses suggest (testing g = 0.37, spacing g = 0.20, interleaving g = 0.40).

## Study explanation

Most laboratory research on retrieval practice, distributed practice and interleaved practice is done with undergraduate students, short word lists, and a test an hour later. That setup is easy to run and hard to transfer. A teacher has a class of thirty teenagers, a curriculum to cover, and an exam in three weeks. The question this paper asks is narrow and practical: do these three techniques still work when the study happens in a real classroom, and if they do, under what conditions do they stop working?

The authors searched for studies of authentic primary and secondary school classrooms published between 2006 and 2024, and built a three-level meta-analysis: 233 effect sizes nested in 71 experiments nested in 53 studies, with 7,303 participants in total. Three levels matter because one classroom study often reports several outcomes (a test right after the lesson, a test at the end of term) and each outcome is a separate effect size. Reading g here: g is a standardized mean difference, so g = 0.37 means the practice group scored 0.37 standard deviations higher than the comparison group on average.

Here is the whole finding in one walk-through. Retrieval practice averaged g = 0.37 (95% CI 0.18 to 0.56), estimated from 120 effect sizes in 31 studies with 3,391 participants. Distributed practice averaged g = 0.20. Interleaved practice averaged g = 0.40 from ten studies with 1,902 students. All three are positive, and all three are smaller than the numbers usually quoted from laboratory syntheses.

A worked example. A biology teacher wants to know whether to build retrieval practice into a unit on cell structure. The moderator results say: use it for factual knowledge, where the classroom effect was clearest at g = 0.53. For conceptual knowledge and for procedural knowledge, retrieval practice showed no significant effect in this pool of classroom studies. So the teacher's plan is a retrieval set on the parts and labels of a cell, where the classroom evidence points the same way as the laboratory evidence, and not a pure "recall the concept of osmosis" routine, where this pool gives no support. For distributed practice, a teacher who compares spaced sessions against one long cram session in the same unit is expecting g = 0.39, while a teacher who already spaces and only changes the spacing formula gets no significant difference. For interleaving, the plan depends on the feedback rule the teacher adopts: without feedback the effect was g = 0.25, with delayed feedback g = 0.82 but from only five effect sizes, and with immediate feedback there was no significant effect.

The most common mistake when reading this paper is to quote the headline average as a rule. "Testing works, g = 0.37" sounds portable, and people read it that way because a single number is easier to carry than a table of moderators. But the paper's own structure says otherwise: the effect depends on the type of knowledge, the posttest format, how well the practice format matches the test format, and the type of learning outcome. A second mistake is to quote a laboratory number as if it were a classroom number. The classroom version is the one with the small average and the sparse moderator cells.

Two connections in this vault. [[Retrieval_Practice|Retrieval practice]] is built on the laboratory finding in Roediger and Karpicke (2006), where testing beat rereading after a delay. This paper is the classroom check on that habit: the technique survives contact with real classrooms, but at a smaller effect and only for some kinds of content, which means a routine built on it should target factual material and be checked rather than assumed. [[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]] treats the same habit as a rule for AI tutors: the assistant asks instead of answering. This paper adds a condition to that rule. If the assistant supplies the answer immediately, the interleaving result here is the warning: with immediate feedback there was no significant effect, while the retrieval result shows no benefit for comprehension or transfer. An assistant that supplies instant answers can make the session feel productive while leaving untouched the parts of learning that this meta-analysis found no support for.

The authors are explicit about the limits. The number of eligible classroom studies is small, the moderator cells are sparse, the unpublished evidence is limited, and they advise against strong conclusions. Read the numbers as a range of what to expect, not a guarantee.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** in authentic classrooms the testing effect averages g = 0.37, the spacing effect g = 0.20 and the interleaving effect g = 0.40, so all three survive contact with real teaching but are weaker than laboratory numbers. The effects are conditional on content type, test format, format matching and learning outcome.
- **Common confusion:** averaging a moderator away. Retrieval practice was clearest for factual knowledge (g = 0.53) and not significant for conceptual or procedural knowledge, so "testing works" is not a content-free claim.
- **Exercise:** pick one unit you teach or study, list the factual items and the conceptual items separately, and decide which half gets a retrieval set and which half gets a different treatment. Then write the feedback rule you will use.

## Results

| Technique | Average effect | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice | g = 0.37 (95% CI 0.18 to 0.56) | 120 effect sizes, 31 studies, 3,391 participants |
| Distributed practice | g = 0.20 | moderated by comparison condition, retention interval, interval ratio, posttest format |
| Interleaved practice | g = 0.40 | ten studies, 1,902 students |

| Boundary condition | Result |
|---|---|
| Retrieval practice, factual knowledge | g = 0.53 |
| Retrieval practice, conceptual or procedural knowledge | no significant effect |
| Retrieval practice on retention | benefit |
| Retrieval practice on comprehension or transfer | no benefit |
| Interleaving without feedback | g = 0.25 |
| Interleaving with delayed feedback | g = 0.82 (only five effect sizes) |
| Interleaving with immediate feedback | no significant effect |
| Distributed practice against massed practice | g = 0.39 |
| Two forms of spaced practice against each other | no significant difference |

## Limitations

- Small number of eligible authentic classroom studies, with sparse moderator cells.
- Limited published evidence, since unpublished classroom studies are not represented.
- The authors advise against strong conclusions from these averages.
- The interleaving estimate with delayed feedback rests on only five effect sizes.
- Interleaving evidence comes from ten studies with 1,902 students, a smaller pool than the retrieval literature.
- Effects for conceptual and procedural knowledge were not significant, so the headline average does not transfer to every kind of content.
