# Distributed practice in classrooms (Mawson and Kang, 2025)

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Question: how large is the classroom effect of spreading study over time instead of massing it, and what changes it?
> Method: meta-analytic review of applied research, screening over 3,000 articles to keep 22 reports with 31 effect sizes and more than 3,000 participants.
> Result: a moderate effect in favor of distributed practice, d = 0.54 (95% CI 0.31 to 0.77), with very high heterogeneity (I2 = 92.13 percent).

## Study explanation

Most people learn by cramming. There is a long evening, an exam in three days, and a decision to read the whole chapter twice. The alternative, spreading the same total study time over several sessions, sounds like it takes more discipline and less time. Mawson and Kang (2025) asked a narrow question about classroom reality: when teachers and students actually use distributed practice, how big is the effect, and what changes it?

The method is a meta-analytic review of applied research. The authors screened over 3,000 articles and kept 22 reports containing 31 effect sizes with more than 3,000 participants. Every study in the pool is applied classroom research, so the question is not whether spacing works in principle, but how large the classroom effect is and how much it varies between classrooms.

The central number is d = 0.54 (95% CI 0.31 to 0.77) in favor of distributed over massed practice. Here d is a standardized mean difference expressed in standard deviations: the distributed practice group scored 0.54 standard deviations higher than the massed group, on average. The confidence interval runs from 0.31 to 0.77, so even the pessimistic end of the interval stays above zero. The other number matters just as much: heterogeneity I2 = 92.13 percent. In plain terms, the studies in this pool disagree with each other almost completely. The average is real, but the spread behind it is enormous. Two classrooms using the same technique, one with a long gap before the test and one with the test the next morning, are not the same classroom.

A worked example. A class is preparing for a unit exam. Version A gives one long review session two days before the exam. Version B splits the same total study time into four shorter sessions spread over the two weeks before the exam. On the meta-analytic average, version B wins by about half a standard deviation, which is why the technique is worth the scheduling work. But that average hides the decisions that set the size. Longer retention intervals went with larger effects, learners at higher education levels went with larger effects, and fewer re-exposures to the material went with larger effects. So the teacher who spaces four sessions across two weeks, with the test at the end, sits in the part of the pool where the effect was largest, while the teacher who spaces two short sessions the day before the exam sits in the part where the estimate is weakest. The review also states what it could not do: a comprehensive quantitative moderator analysis was not possible because of the number of studies. The three patterns above are observed associations, not tested moderator models.

The most common mistake is to quote d = 0.54 as the classroom effect of spacing. The mistake happens because a single average is quotable and the heterogeneity statistic is not. A second mistake is the opposite one: reading the huge spread as if spacing is unreliable. The I2 value says the size depends on conditions, not that the direction is in doubt.

Two connections. [[Retrieval_Practice|Retrieval practice]] is the other half of the same toolkit, and this note supplies the timing rule the concept note leans on: the gap between practice sessions, and the gap between the last session and the test, are what set the effect size. [[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]] is where the scheduling happens in practice, because an assistant can hand you a short review at spaced intervals instead of one long summary, and can re-ask you the same question a week later. This note also warns about the tempting shortcut: if the assistant re-exposes you to the same material every day, you sit in the low effect part of the pool, where fewer re-exposures went with larger effects.

Keep the limits in mind. Heterogeneity is very high, a proper moderator analysis could not be run, the pool is 22 reports of applied research only, and the education level pattern means the estimate may not transfer to primary school.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** spreading study over time beats massing it in classrooms by a moderate amount, d = 0.54 (95% CI 0.31 to 0.77). The size varies more than the direction does, with heterogeneity I2 = 92.13 percent.
- **Common confusion:** the average effect and the spread of effects are different facts, and quoting d = 0.54 alone hides the fact that two classrooms can differ a lot.
- **Exercise:** take one topic you plan to study this week and write down the sessions you would spread it over, the gap between the last session and the test, and how many times the material is re-exposed. Then compare that gap with a single cram session.

## Limitations

- Heterogeneity is very high, I2 = 92.13 percent, so the average effect size describes the pool poorly on its own.
- A comprehensive quantitative moderator analysis was not possible because of the number of studies, so the three patterns are associations rather than tested moderators.
- The pool is 22 reports from applied research only, so it does not cover laboratory studies of spacing.
- The education level pattern suggests the estimate may not transfer to primary school classrooms.
- The number of re-exposures was associated with effect size, but the direction of that association does not tell you how many re-exposures is enough.
