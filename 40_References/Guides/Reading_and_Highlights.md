# Reading and highlights

> [!tldr] In 3 lines
> Highlighting and rereading feel productive, but a large review rated both low in usefulness for learning.
> What helps instead: ask questions before you read, close the book after each section and recall it, and explain the hard parts in your own words.
> In this vault a highlight is a pointer to a source, never the note itself; the note is what you wrote closed book.

## Study explanation

The problem is a familiar one. You read a chapter with a highlighter, the page turns yellow, you reread the yellow parts before the test, and two weeks later little is left. Dunlosky and colleagues reviewed ten common study techniques and rated how well each one is supported across learners, materials and tests ([F1]). Highlighting and rereading got a low rating. That does not mean they never help. It means the evidence that they help reliably is weak, while two other techniques, practice testing and distributed practice, got the top rating.

Why does the highlighter fool you? Marking a sentence takes a decision about the text, not a recall of it. Rereading makes the text familiar, and familiarity feels like knowing. The same trap appears in [[Retrieval_Practice|retrieval practice]]: in the experiment behind it, rereading won at five minutes and lost at one week ([F2]).

So what do you do with a book? Three moves, each backed by the evidence in [[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]].

First, ask before you read. Turn the chapter title and headings into questions and guess the answers. A wrong guess is fine: guessing before you study helps you learn the answer later, the prequestion effect described in that guide.

Second, read one section, then close the book. Write what you remember in a few lines, then open the book and check. What you could not recall is exactly what to review, and each gap becomes a card.

Third, explain the hard parts. When a passage does not make sense, write why each step follows from the one before. Self-explanation is one of the techniques with support in that guide.

A worked example. A book track, chapter 3, on how memory consolidates during sleep. Before reading, you write three questions from the headings: what consolidation is, which sleep stage matters, and what happens without sleep. You guess two of them wrong. You read the first section, close the book, and write four lines. On checking, you got the main claim but missed the study the author uses as evidence. That gap becomes a card, with the page number as its source. You highlight one sentence only: the definition you will want to quote, so you can find the page again.

The most common mistake is to treat highlights as notes. A page of highlighted quotes is the author's words in the author's order, so reviewing it is rereading again. The second mistake is to copy whole paragraphs into the vault. The note you keep should be your recall, checked and corrected, with page numbers for anything you quote.

Highlights still have one good use: finding things again. If your reader app exports highlights, keep them as a source list in the session note, each with its page or location, and write your own summary above them, closed book.

## Key takeaways

- **Remember:** highlighting and rereading were rated low; practice testing and spacing were rated high. A highlight points to a source, it does not teach you the source.
- **Common confusion:** recognizing a highlighted sentence is not the same as recalling it. Only recall shows what you know.
- **Exercise:** take the last chapter you highlighted. Close the book, write what you remember in five lines, then check it against the highlights and count the gaps.

## Reading routine in this vault

1. **Before:** in the session note, turn the headings into questions and write a guess for each. The `pretest` skill asks them for you.
2. **During:** one section at a time. Close the book and write a short recall under `## Recall without notes` in your own words.
3. **Check:** open the book, correct the recall, and add the page number to every claim.
4. **Cards:** each gap becomes a `question::answer` card with the page as its source.
5. **Highlights:** optional, kept under `## Highlights` as quotes with page numbers. Never the study layer.

## Not verified

- The review's ratings summarize evidence up to 2013; newer studies of digital highlighting were not checked here.
- The size of the prequestion and self-explanation benefits comes from the sources cited in [[Studying_with_AI|Studying with AI]], which lists what was and was not read in full.
